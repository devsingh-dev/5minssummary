Quantum Entanglement Explained: How Two Particles Share One Fate Across the Universe

Albert Einstein doubted that measuring one particle could instantly define another light-years away. How Bell’s Theorem and Nobel Prize-winning experiments proved quantum mechanics right.

5minSummary Editorial

In 1935, Albert Einstein, along with physicists Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, published one of the most famous papers in the history of physics, known as the EPR Paradox (*Physical Review*, May 1935). Although Einstein had helped launch quantum mechanics thirty years earlier with his discovery of the photoelectric effect, he was deeply troubled by a bizarre mathematical prediction buried inside the new quantum equations—a phenomenon that Austrian physicist Erwin Schrödinger soon named Entanglement (*Verschränkung*).

According to quantum mechanics, if two subatomic particles (such as two photons or two electrons) interact in a specific way and then fly apart in opposite directions—even to opposite ends of the galaxy—they cannot be described as two separate objects. Instead, they remain bound together in a single shared mathematical wave function. Measure the quantum spin or polarization of Particle A on Earth, and the state of Particle B light-years away becomes fixed at that exact instant. To Einstein, whose theory of Special Relativity forbade any physical influence from traveling faster than light, this violated local realism—leading him to argue that quantum mechanics must be incomplete.

The Glove Analogy: Local Hidden Variables vs. Quantum Superposition

To understand why Einstein and quantum pioneer Niels Bohr disagreed so fiercely, consider a simple everyday analogy involving a pair of shoes or gloves:

  • Einstein’s “Hidden Variables” View (Classical Common Sense): Suppose you place a left-handed glove inside Box A and a right-handed glove inside Box B, shuffle the boxes, keep Box A in New York, and fly Box B to Tokyo. The moment you open Box A in New York and see a left glove, you instantly know that Box B in Tokyo contains the right glove. Nothing magical traveled faster than light between New York and Tokyo—Box A *always* held the left glove from the very beginning; you just did not know it until you looked. Einstein believed entangled particles worked the exact same way, carrying pre-determined instructions called Local Hidden Variables.
  • Niels Bohr’s Quantum Superposition View (Copenhagen Interpretation): Quantum mechanics claimed something radically stranger: before you open Box A, neither glove is left-handed or right-handed! Both gloves exist in a probabilistic blur of both left and right simultaneously (Quantum Superposition). It is the physical act of measuring Box A in New York that forces the particle to choose a state—and at that exact moment, Box B in Tokyo collapses into the opposite state.

1964: John Stewart Bell Turns Philosophy Into an Experiment

For nearly thirty years, physicists assumed there was no experimental way to test whether Einstein’s pre-determined Hidden Variables or Bohr’s Quantum Superposition was right, since you cannot peek at a particle before measuring it. Then in 1964, Northern Irish physicist John Stewart Bell, working on leave from CERN, derived a brilliant mathematical theorem known as Bell’s Inequality.

Bell realized that if you measure pairs of entangled particles at intermediate detector angles (such as 22.5 degrees or 45 degrees rather than just 0 and 90 degrees), pre-determined classical Hidden Variables have a strict mathematical upper limit on how strongly the two detectors’ results can correlate. Quantum entanglement predicts correlations stronger than any local hidden variable theory could ever fake.

The 2022 Nobel Prize Experiments That Settled the Debate

Over the next five decades, three pioneering experimental physicists—John Clauser, Alain Aspect, and Anton Zeilinger (who jointly won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics)—built increasingly sophisticated experiments firing entangled photons through polarization detectors:

  • 1. John Clauser (1972): Using calcium atoms emitting entangled photons at UC Berkeley, Clauser conducted the first real laboratory test of Bell’s Inequality—and found that the photons violated Bell’s classical limit, matching quantum mechanics.
  • 2. Alain Aspect (1982): Critics wondered if the detectors in Clauser’s lab were somehow signaling each other beforehand. At the University of Paris-Sud, Aspect used acoustic-optical switches that changed the detector orientations every 10 nanoseconds while the photons were already in flight—faster than a light signal could travel between the detectors. Quantum entanglement still won.
  • 3. Anton Zeilinger & Loophole-Free Tests (1998–2017): Zeilinger’s group in Vienna even used the random light arriving from distant cosmic quasars billions of light-years away to set the detector angles at the last microsecond, proving beyond a shadow of a doubt that Local Hidden Variables do not exist. Nature is genuinely non-local.

Why Entanglement Does NOT Violate the Speed of Light (No-Communication Theorem)

If measuring Particle A instantly correlates with Particle B across light-years, can we use quantum entanglement to send faster-than-light Morse code messages to Mars? No—and here is why Einstein’s speed limit remains safe:

Because the outcome of measuring Particle A is 100% random (a 50/50 quantum coin flip that you cannot force to land heads or tails), the observer holding Particle B at Mars simply sees a random stream of 50/50 outcomes. Only when the two observers later compare their measurement logs over a normal, light-speed radio channel do they see the spooky correlation. Known as the No-Communication Theorem, this ensures information never travels faster than light.

Instead, engineers today use quantum entanglement as the backbone of Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)—such as China’s *Micius* quantum satellite—where any attempt by a hacker to eavesdrop on an entangled photon stream immediately breaks the quantum superposition and alerts both sender and receiver.
