How the British East India Company Ruled India

In 1600, India generated nearly a quarter of the world’s GDP while Britain was a minor island economy. How a private London corporation with a thirty-man office conquered a subcontinent.

E

Editor

5minSummary Editorial

Imagine a modern multinational corporation—complete with a board of directors, quarterly shareholder meetings, and stock certificates traded in London—that somehow commands its own private army of 260,000 soldiers (twice the size of the official British Army), mints its own silver currency, runs its own courts and prisons, wages wars of annexation, and collects taxes from two hundred million people. It sounds like a dystopian novel, yet for a hundred years between 1757 and 1858, that was the literal reality of how the British East India Company (EIC) ruled the Indian subcontinent.

When Queen Elizabeth I signed a royal charter on December 31, 1600, granting a monopoly on Asian trade to a group of 218 London merchants operating out of a small five-window building on Leadenhall Street, the balance of global power was the exact opposite of what it would become two centuries later. In 1600, Britain produced less than 2% of world GDP, while Mughal India under Emperor Akbar produced roughly 22% to 25% of global economic output—serving as the world’s premier manufacturer of fine cotton textiles, muslins, spices, indigo, and saltpeter.

How did a group of foreign traders who arrived on Indian shores begging for permission to rent coastal warehouses end up ruling the richest empire on Earth?

Phase 1: Humble Traders at the Mughal Court (1600–1707)

For the first 150 years of its existence, the East India Company had neither the power nor the realistic ambition to conquer interior India. When the Company’s first ambassador, Sir Thomas Roe, arrived at the dazzling court of Mughal Emperor Jahangir at Ajmer in 1615, he was treated as a minor envoy from a distant island. All the Company wanted was a royal *farman* (imperial permit) to establish coastal trading posts—called “factories”—at Surat (1612), Madras (1639), Bombay (1668), and Calcutta (1690).

When the Company foolishly tried to challenge Mughal authority by force during Child’s War in 1686, Emperor Aurangzeb’s armies crushed the British effortlessly, forcing Company envoys to prostrate themselves on the floor before the throne and pay a heavy indemnity to get their trading privileges back.

Phase 2: The Power Vacuum and Corporate Militarization (1707–1757)

Everything changed after the death of Emperor Aurangzeb in 1707. Overextended by decades of war in the Deccan, the centralized Mughal Empire fractured rapidly. Powerful regional governors (*Nawabs* and *Nizams*) broke away to form independent successor states in Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, and Carnatic, while the Maratha Confederacy expanded aggressively across central and northern India, and the Persian ruler Nadir Shah sacked Delhi in 1739.

Into this fragmented political landscape stepped the European trading companies—principally the British EIC and the French *Compagnie des Indes*. The French governor Joseph François Dupleixand his British rival Robert Clive discovered a fateful military and political formula:

  • Training Indian Sepoys in European Volley-Fire Drill: Both companies recruited tens of thousands of local Indian professional soldiers (sepoys, from the Persian *sipahi*), equipped them with flintlock muskets and mobile field artillery, and paid them regular monthly cash wages.
  • Renting Armies to Rival Indian Princes: Whenever two claimants fought for a regional throne in the Carnatic or Deccan, the Company offered its disciplined sepoy battalions to one side in exchange for massive cash payments and tax-free land grants (*jagirs*). Indian rulers were effectively paying for the buildup of the very corporate army that would soon overthrow them.
“India was not conquered by a foreign army shipped from Britain; it was conquered by an Indian sepoy army funded by Indian bankers and commanded by a London boardroom.” — Historical Consensus

The Turning Point: Plassey (1757) and the Diwani of Bengal (1765)

The richest province on the subcontinent was Bengal, whose fertile delta and millions of skilled weavers generated immense wealth. When the young Nawab of Bengal, Siraj-ud-Daulah, captured Calcutta in 1756 to stop the Company from illegally fortifying the city and abusing tax-free trade passes (*dastaks*), the Company dispatched Robert Clive northward from Madras.

What happened at the Battle of Plassey on June 23, 1757, was less a military battle than a boardroom coup and banking conspiracy. Before a single cannon was fired, Clive struck a secret deal with Mir Jafar (the commander-in-chief of the Nawab’s army) and the Jagat Seths (the powerful Marwari banking family of Murshidabad who financed the Bengal court). During the battle, Mir Jafar held the bulk of the Bengali army back without fighting. Siraj-ud-Daulah was overthrown, Mir Jafar was installed as a puppet Nawab, and the Bengal treasury at Murshidabad was emptied onto barges headed for the Company’s vaults in Calcutta.

Eight years later, after defeating the combined forces of Bengal, Awadh, and the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II at the Battle of Buxar (1764), Clive forced the Emperor to sign the Treaty of Allahabad (1765). With a stroke of a pen, the East India Company was appointed the Diwan (Imperial Tax Collector) of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.

How the “Drain of Wealth” Worked

The granting of the *Diwani* in 1765 completely inverted the economics of global trade:

  • Before 1765: To buy Indian cotton textiles and spices, the East India Company had to ship tons of silver bullion *from London to India*, enriching the Indian economy.
  • After 1765: The Company collected over three million pounds sterling a year in land revenue taxes directly from Indian peasant farmers, used a portion of that Indian tax money to buy Indian textiles at forced below-market prices from local weavers, shipped those goods to Europe and China for massive profits, and pocketed the difference—without bringing a single ounce of silver from Britain!

This relentless revenue extraction—famously analyzed a century later by Dadabhai Naoroji as the *“Drain of Wealth”*—had catastrophic human consequences. When the monsoon failed in 1770, the Company refused to lower land taxes (in fact, it raised tax collections by 10% during the famine to protect shareholder dividends), resulting in the Great Bengal Famine of 1770, which claimed the lives of an estimated ten million people—nearly one-third of Bengal’s population.

Subsidiary Alliances and the Doctrine of Lapse

Over the next eighty years, through wars against Tipu Sultan of Mysore, the Anglo-Maratha Wars, and the Anglo-Sikh Wars, the Company swallowed up the rest of the subcontinent using two legal traps:

  • Lord Wellesley’s Subsidiary Alliance: Indian princely states were forced to disband their own armies, accept a permanent British military garrison inside their capital, and hand over land or cash to pay for those troops—turning proud kingdoms into disarmed protectorates.
  • Lord Dalhousie’s Doctrine of Lapse (1848–1856): The Company decreed that if an Indian ruler died without a natural biological male heir—overriding the ancient Hindu legal right of adopting a successor—the kingdom would automatically “lapse” and be annexed by the Company. Using this pretext, the Company seized Satara, Sambalpur, Udaipur, Nagpur, and Jhansi, and finally annexed Awadh in 1856.

1857: The Great Rebellion and the End of the Company

By 1857, resentment across every layer of Indian society—dispossessed queens like Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi, overtaxed farmers, ruined artisans, and the Company’s own Indian sepoys—reached a boiling point. Triggered by the introduction of the new Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle (whose paper cartridges were greased with beef and pork tallow, offending both Hindu and Muslim soldiers), the Revolt of 1857 erupted from Meerut to Delhi, Kanpur, and Lucknow.

Although the Company suppressed the uprising after more than a year of brutal fighting, the British Parliament realized that a profit-driven trading company could never again be trusted to govern a continent. Under the Government of India Act of August 1858, the East India Company was stripped of all its territories and armies, and rule was transferred directly to the British Crown (The British Raj)—leaving behind a permanent historical warning about what happens when corporate profit is handed sovereign power without accountability.
