How Does the Global Banking System Work?

When you deposit ₹10,000 in a bank, it does not sit inside a steel vault. Here is how commercial banks create money through loans, how central banks steer inflation, and how trillions cross borders daily.

5minSummary Editorial

Open your smartphone banking app and look at your account balance. When you see ₹25,000 or $1,000 displayed on the screen, it is natural to picture a stack of crisp paper currency notes with your name on a tag sitting inside a heavy steel vault at your local bank branch. Yet if every citizen in your city walked into their bank branch at 10:00 AM tomorrow morning and asked to withdraw their entire balance in physical cash, not a single bank on Earth could hand over the money. Why? Because less than five to ten percent of the money in the modern world exists as physical paper notes and coins.

Over ninety percent of the global money supply exists purely as digital ledger entries—numbers typed into encrypted computer databases maintained by commercial banks and central banks. Far from being passive storage lockers for paper cash, banks are the circulatory system of the modern economy, turning idle savings into homes, factories, and infrastructure while settling trillions of dollars across continents every single second.

How Commercial Banks Actually Make Money (And Create Credit)

At the most basic level, a commercial bank (like State Bank of India, HDFC, JPMorgan Chase, or Barclays) acts as a financial bridge between two groups of people: savers (who have money they don’t need to spend today) and borrowers (who need capital today to buy a home, purchase farm machinery, or expand a company).

This bridge runs on the Net Interest Margin (The Spread):

  • Paying Savers: You deposit ₹1,00,000 into a savings account or fixed deposit, and the bank pays you, say, 4% to 7% annual interest.
  • Lending to Borrowers: The bank lends that capital out as a Home Loan or Business Loan at 9% to 12% annual interest. The difference between the interest the bank collects from borrowers and the interest it pays to depositors pays for the bank’s servers, employees, and profit.

Fractional Reserve Banking and Money Creation

Here is the part of banking that surprises almost everyone: banks do not wait for physical cash to be carried into a vault before issuing a loan; making a loan actually creates new bank-deposit money in the economy.

Under Fractional Reserve Banking, regulators require a bank to keep only a small fraction of its deposits (say, 10% in liquid reserves and central bank balances) to handle everyday ATM withdrawals and transfers, while lending out the remaining 90%.

Follow the math of what happens when ₹1,00,000 is deposited into Bank A:

  • Step 1: Bank A keeps ₹10,000 (10%) in reserve and lends ₹90,000 to a carpenter named Rohan to buy woodworking machines.
  • Step 2: Rohan pays that ₹90,000 to the machinery seller, who deposits the ₹90,000 into Bank B.
  • Step 3: Bank B keeps 10% (₹9,000) in reserve and lends out the remaining ₹81,000 to a shopkeeper to buy inventory.

Look at what just happened: your original ₹1,00,000 deposit is still on your screen, the machinery seller has ₹90,000 on their screen, and the shopkeeper has ₹81,000! Through this Money Multiplier effect, an initial deposit ripples through the banking system to finance multiple layers of real economic activity—all backed by the legal promise of borrowers to repay their loans with future work.

“Modern money is an ecosystem of trust. It works as long as borrowers repay their loans, banks maintain prudent capital buffers, and central banks guard the value of the currency.” — Monetary Economics Primer

The Conductor of the Orchestra: What Central Banks Do

If commercial banks can expand credit through lending, what stops them from creating too much money and triggering runaway inflation, or panicking during a crisis? Sitting at the apex of every nation’s financial system is a Central Bank—such as the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), the U.S. Federal Reserve (The Fed), the European Central Bank (ECB), or the Bank of England.

A central bank is the “Banker’s Bank” and the guardian of economic stability, wielding three powerful levers:

1. Setting Interest Rates (Repo Rate / Policy Rate)

The Repo Rate is the benchmark interest rate at which the central bank lends short-term funds to commercial banks. It acts like the gas pedal and brake pedal for the entire national economy:

  • When Inflation Is Too High (Applying the Brakes): If prices of food, fuel, and housing are rising too fast because too much money is chasing too few goods, the central bank raises the Repo Rate. Commercial banks immediately raise their EMI interest rates for home, car, and business loans. Borrowing slows down, spending cools off, and inflation comes back under control.
  • When the Economy Is Slowing (Pressing the Gas Pedal): During a recession or downturn, the central bank cuts interest rates, making loans cheaper so businesses hire workers and families buy homes.

2. Reserve Requirements (CRR and SLR)

Central banks mandate the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)—the exact percentage of deposits every commercial bank must lock away in cash with the central bank or in ultra-safe government bonds, ensuring banks always have a safety cushion.

3. Lender of Last Resort and Deposit Insurance

If a rumor causes panicked customers to rush to a bank all at once (a bank run), even a healthy bank can run out of immediate cash because its money is tied up in 20-year home loans. The central bank steps in as the Lender of Last Resort, providing emergency liquidity against the bank’s solid loan assets—while government Deposit Insurance (like DICGC in India covering up to ₹5 Lakh per depositor, or FDIC in the US covering $250,000) reassures ordinary citizens that their hard-earned savings are safe.

How Money Crosses Borders: Clearing Houses, SWIFT, and Nostro Accounts

Finally, how does money move when you send a payment from one bank to another—or from a business in Bengaluru to a supplier in Frankfurt or New York?

  • Domestic Settlement (RTGS, NEFT, and UPI): Every commercial bank holds a master settlement account at its national central bank. When you send ₹500 via UPI from your SBI account to a friend’s HDFC account, no physical ₹500 note travels on a truck between the two banks. Instead, at the central clearing house, the RBI’s computer simply subtracts ₹500 from SBI’s master balance and adds ₹500 to HDFC’s master balance in milliseconds.
  • International Transfers and SWIFT: Contrary to popular belief, SWIFT (*Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication*), headquartered in Belgium and linking over 11,000 banks across 200 countries, does not actually hold or move money! SWIFT is an ultra-secure encrypted messaging network. When an Indian bank sends $10,000 to a German bank, it sends a standardized SWIFT message instructing correspondent banks (where banks hold foreign-currency Nostro and Vostro accounts) to debit one ledger and credit the other.
  • The Bank for International Settlements (BIS): Based in Basel, Switzerland, the BIS acts as the “Central Bank for Central Banks,” setting the global Basel III capital rules that force every major bank in the world to hold enough protective capital to survive financial storms.
