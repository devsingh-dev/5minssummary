Why Are Some Countries Rich and Others Poor?

Why do two towns separated by a single border fence have a tenfold difference in income? Economics reveals why institutions, property rights, and human capital matter more than gold or oil.

E

Editor

5minSummary Editorial

Look at a satellite photograph of the Korean Peninsula taken at night. South of the 38th parallel, South Korea blazes like a constellation of electric light—home to one of the highest standards of living, longest life expectancies, and most advanced technology sectors on Earth. Immediately north of the border line, North Korea sits in almost complete darkness, with an average citizen earning less than one-twentieth of their southern cousin’s income. Or travel to the town of Nogales, cut in half by a border fence between Arizona (USA) and Sonora (Mexico): same climate, same geography, same ancestral culture, yet households on the northern side of the fence enjoy three times the income, superior public health, and vastly safer streets.

What explains the staggering gap between rich and poor nations? Today, a citizen in the world’s richest countries earns more than thirty to fifty times the annual income of a citizen in the poorest nations. For over two centuries—since Adam Smith published *The Wealth of Nations* in 1776—economists, historians, and geographers have wrestled with this fundamental puzzle.

Debunking the Three Oldest Myths About National Wealth

Before looking at what actually drives prosperity, let us examine three popular explanations that fail when tested against real-world data:

Myth 1: “Rich Countries Are Rich Because They Have Natural Resources”

Intuition suggests that a country sitting on vast reserves of gold, diamonds, oil, and fertile minerals should automatically be wealthy. In reality, development economists have documented the exact opposite phenomenon—the “Resource Curse” (or Paradox of Plenty).

Some of the wealthiest, most innovative societies on the planet—such as Japan, Singapore, Switzerland, and South Korea—possess almost zero oil, minimal mineral deposits, and limited arable land. Meanwhile, nations blessed with immense oil and diamond wealth—like Venezuela or the Democratic Republic of the Congo—have suffered decades of poverty and instability. When a government can pump money straight out of the ground without taxing or educating its citizens, rulers have little incentive to build good schools, fair courts, or diversified industries.

Myth 2: “Geography and Climate Determine Everything”

Geography certainly plays a real supporting role. As economist Jeffrey Sachs and historian Jared Diamond pointed out, landlocked countries without navigable rivers face much higher shipping costs to reach global markets, and tropical regions historically carried a heavier burden of vector-borne diseases like malaria. Yet geography cannot be destiny: tropical Singapore is one of the richest nations on Earth, desert Botswana has been one of Africa’s fastest-growing success stories since independence, and North and South Korea share the exact same peninsula and climate.

Myth 3: “Culture Alone Explains Economic Success”

For decades, sociologists tried to credit national wealth to specific religious or cultural traits. Yet when East and West Germany were divided between 1945 and 1989—or when China reformed its economic rules after 1978, and India dismantled the *License Raj* in 1991—the exact same people with the exact same cultural traditions suddenly multiplied their economic output tenfold within a single generation once the rules of the economy changed.

“Nations thrive when they build economic and political institutions that reward creation, innovation, and education rather than extraction and rent-seeking.” — Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences Committee (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson)

The Real Engine: Inclusive vs. Extractive Institutions

In landmark research that earned the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences, economists Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James A. Robinson demonstrated that the decisive difference between rich and poor nations lies in the quality of their institutions—the human-made rules, laws, and incentives that govern how an economy works:

  • Extractive Institutions (Why Nations Stay Poor): In an extractive system, political power is concentrated in the hands of a narrow elite without checks and balances. Property rights are insecure; courts are corrupt; monopolies are handed to politically connected cronies; and ordinary citizens know that if they work hard, invent a better tool, or build a successful business, their profits can be arbitrarily confiscated or extorted. Under extractive rules, talent withers or emigrates.
  • Inclusive Institutions (Why Nations Grow Rich): Inclusive institutions establish a level playing field protected by the Rule of Law. Even the president or prime minister is subject to the courts; private property and contracts are enforced fairly; anyone can start a business without paying bribes; patents protect inventors; and public infrastructure and schools allow millions of ordinary citizens to participate in the economy.

The 4 Pillars That Turn a Developing Nation Into a Prosperous One

When we trace the historical trajectory of every country that successfully leaped from poverty to prosperity over the past century, four practical pillars appear every single time:

1. Secure Property Rights and Contract Enforcement

Peruvian economist Hernando de Soto pointed out that poor people in developing countries actually own trillions of dollars in land, homes, and small workshops—but because they lack formal, legally protected land titles, they hold “dead capital.” They cannot use their land as collateral to get a low-interest bank loan to expand a business. When a country makes property registration fast, digital, and incorruptible, dead assets come alive.

2. High-Value Human Capital (Health and Practical Education)

When raw natural resources account for less than ten percent of modern wealth, the greatest asset of any nation sits between the ears of its citizens. Investing in universal primary healthcare, childhood nutrition, female literacy, and rigorous mathematics, science, and vocational education turns a large population into a massive engine of innovation.

3. Infrastructure That Lowers Transaction Friction

Consider how much wealth is destroyed when a farmer’s vegetables rot in a truck stuck on a potholed road, or when a small workshop loses four hours a day to power cuts. Reliable 24/7 electricity, high-speed ports and highways, cold-storage supply chains, and instant digital payment rails (like India’s UPI digital public infrastructure) slash the friction of doing business for millions of small entrepreneurs simultaneously.

4. Openness to Trade and Creative Destruction

Sustained growth requires what economist Joseph Schumpeter called creative destruction—allowing new technologies, more efficient methods, and young startups to replace outdated, inefficient monopolies. Nations that plug into global trade networks, welcome technological learning, and maintain stable, low-inflation monetary policies compound their productivity year after year.

Prosperity Is a Choice Built Over Decades

The most hopeful takeaway from modern economics is that poverty is not an immutable curse written in a country’s soil or stars. Because institutions, laws, education systems, and infrastructure are built by human beings, any society that protects the rule of law, invests in its people, and rewards honest enterprise can rewrite its economic destiny within a single lifetime.
