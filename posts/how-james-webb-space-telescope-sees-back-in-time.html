How the James Webb Space Telescope Sees 13.5 Billion Years Back in Time

Why studying the earliest galaxies in the universe required building a gold-coated infrared observatory parked 1.5 million kilometers from Earth at minus 233 degrees Celsius.

E

Editor

5minSummary Editorial

On Christmas Day, December 25, 2021, an Ariane 5 rocket lifted off from French Guiana carrying the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)—a $10 billion international collaboration between NASA, the European Space Agency (ESA), and the Canadian Space Agency (CSA). Over the next 29 days, as the spacecraft traveled 1.5 million kilometers away from Earth, it executed 344 single-point-failure mechanical deployments in the vacuum of space, unfolding a five-layer tennis-court-sized sunshield and snapping 18 hexagonal gold-coated mirror segments into alignment with nanometer precision.

Since releasing its first deep-field images in July 2022, JWST has routinely spotted galaxies that formed just 290 to 400 million years after the Big Bang—looking more than 13.4 billion years into the cosmic past. How is a telescope physically able to look backward in time, and why could the famous Hubble Space Telescope not see these earliest stars?

The Finite Speed of Light and Cosmic Redshift

In physics, looking across space is automatically looking backward in time because light travels at a finite speed of 299,792 kilometers per second in a vacuum. Sunlight takes 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach your eyes; light from the nearest star system, Alpha Centauri, takes 4.37 years; and light from the Andromeda Galaxy takes 2.5 million years.

However, seeing objects 13.5 billion light-years away involves a second, far more challenging physical phenomenon: Cosmological Redshift. When the first stars and galaxies ignited after the Big Bang, they emitted brilliant ultraviolet and visible light. As that light traveled toward Earth for over 13 billion years, the expansion of space itself stretched the light waves along the way:

  • Short wavelengths (Ultraviolet & Blue Visible Light): Approximately 100 to 500 nanometers when emitted by young, hot stars.
  • Stretched wavelengths (Near- and Mid-Infrared Light): By the time those ancient light waves reach us today, cosmic expansion has stretched their wavelengths by a factor of 10 to 15 (Redshift z = 10 to 14), shifting them completely out of the visible spectrum and into the infrared spectrum (0.6 to 28 microns).

Because the Hubble Space Telescope was designed primarily to observe ultraviolet and visible light (with only limited near-infrared capability up to 1.7 microns), the earliest galaxies were literally invisible to Hubble’s cameras. JWST was engineered from the ground up as an infrared observatory.

Why JWST Uses Gold-Coated Beryllium Mirrors

Unlike Hubble’s single 2.4-meter glass mirror, JWST carries a 6.5-meter primary mirror composed of 18 hexagonal segments, giving it more than 6.25 times the light-collecting area of Hubble. Engineers chose two specific materials for those segments:

  • Solid Beryllium Base: Each mirror segment is milled from lightweight beryllium, a metal that holds its exact geometric shape at cryogenic temperatures close to absolute zero without warping.
  • Microscopic 24-Karat Gold Coating: While silver or aluminum reflects visible light well, pure gold reflects 98% of infrared light. Across all 18 mirror segments, engineers vapor-deposited a layer of gold just 100 nanometers thick—requiring only 48.25 grams (about the weight of a golf ball) of total gold for the entire 6.5-meter mirror!

Operating at Minus 233°C at Lagrange Point L2

Observing faint infrared signals presents a massive thermodynamic problem: every warm object emits its own infrared heat radiation. If JWST were warm, the heat glowing off its own mirrors and electronics would blind its infrared sensors, like trying to photograph a firefly next to a floodlight.

To stay frigidly cold, JWST does not orbit Earth like Hubble (where heat radiating off Earth’s surface warms the spacecraft every 90 minutes). Instead, JWST orbits the Sun at the Second Sun-Earth Lagrange Point (L2), located 1.5 million kilometers (nearly 4 times the distance to the Moon) directly behind Earth as viewed from the Sun. There, its five-layer Kapton sunshield blocks heat from the Sun, Earth, and Moon simultaneously:

  • Hot (Sun-Facing) Side: Reaches +85°C (185°F) in constant sunlight.
  • Cold (Deep-Space) Side: Plummets to −233°C (−388°F, or just 40 Kelvin above absolute zero), allowing the NIRCam, NIRSpec, and MIRI instruments (cooled even further to 7 Kelvin by a helium cryocooler) to detect the faintest infrared photons in the cosmos.

Atmospheric Spectroscopy: Reading the Air of Alien Worlds

Beyond peering at early galaxies, JWST is revolutionizing exoplanet science through transmission spectroscopy. When a distant exoplanet passes in front of its parent star, a tiny fraction of starlight filters through the planet’s atmosphere. Because every chemical molecule absorbs specific infrared wavelengths like a barcode, JWST has already detected water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and sulfur dioxide in the atmospheres of distant worlds—opening a new era in the search for habitable environments beyond our solar system.
