When we were not alone: The forgotten human species that shared Earth with Homo sapiens

Just 60,000 years ago, at least five distinct species of humans walked the planet at the same time. How ancient DNA revealed our lost cousins—and why only we survived.

E

Editor

5minSummary Editorial

Look around the world today and human uniqueness seems absolute. While there are multiple species of bears (polar bears, grizzly bears, sun bears, sloth bears), multiple species of big cats (lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars), and dozens of species of monkeys, there is only one single species of human—Homo sapiens—inhabiting every continent from the Arctic Circle to the tip of South America. Because we have had the planet to ourselves for the entirety of recorded history, we naturally assume it was always meant to be that way.

Remember the famous “March of Progress” illustration printed in twentieth-century schoolbooks: a neat, single-file line of stooping apes standing up taller and taller until they turn into modern humans. Modern paleoanthropology and ancient genomics have completely demolished that single-file line. Evolution was never a ladder leading inevitably to us; it was a lush, branching bush.

As recently as 60,000 to 100,000 years ago—a mere blink of an eye in geological time—*Homo sapiens* was not alone. At least five or six distinct human species walked the Earth at the exact same time, making stone tools, controlling fire, and adapting to wildly different environments.

Meet Our Lost Human Cousins

Imagine taking a global tour of Planet Earth around 75,000 years ago. Depending on which continent or island you visited, you would have encountered remarkably different human beings:

1. Homo neanderthalensis (The Neanderthals of Eurasia)

Inhabiting Europe, the Middle East, and Western Asia for over 300,000 years, Neanderthals were anything but thedim-witted cave brutes of early nineteenth-century caricature. They were stocky, barrel-chested, cold-adapted humans with tremendous muscular strength and brains that were on average slightly larger than ours (1,450 cubic centimeters vs. 1,350 cc). Archaeological sites prove that Neanderthals cared for injured and elderly clan members for decades, buried their dead, crafted birch-bark tar adhesives requiring precise underground temperature control, wore eagle-talon jewelry, and painted geometric red ochre pigments on cave walls in Spain more than 64,000 years ago.

2. The Denisovans (The Ghost Lineage of Asia)

In 2008, Russian archaeologists digging in Denisova Cave in the Altai Mountains of Siberia found a tiny fossilized pinky-finger bone belonging to a young girl who lived around 50,000 years ago. When geneticist Svante Pääbo and his team at the Max Planck Institute extracted and sequenced the ancient DNA preserved inside that tiny bone in 2010, they received the shock of their careers: the DNA belonged neither to *Homo sapiens* nor to Neanderthals. For the first time in history, a brand-new archaic human species—the Denisovans—had been discovered not from a complete skull, but from a strand of DNA!

Subsequent discoveries—including a massive jawbone with giant molars found at 3,280 meters altitude on the Tibetan Plateau (Baishiya Karst Cave) and fossils in Laos—revealed that Denisovans ranged all across East Asia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia, adapting to everything from freezing high-altitude mountains to tropical rainforests.

3. Homo floresiensis (The “Hobbits” of Indonesia) and Homo luzonensis

On the isolated Indonesian island of Flores, paleontologists in 2003 uncovered the skeletal remains of Homo floresiensis—diminutive humans standing just 1.06 meters (3 feet 6 inches) tall, shaped by island dwarfism (an evolutionary process where large mammals stranded on islands with limited food and no large predators shrink over generations, while small creatures like Komodo dragons grow giant). Even though their brains were only a third the size of ours, they butchered dwarf Stegodon elephants with stone tools and survived until roughly 50,000 years ago. Thousands of miles north in the Philippines, another miniature human species, Homo luzonensis, thrived during the same era.

4. Homo erectus (The Longest-Surviving Traveler)

Before we get too proud of *Homo sapiens*—who have existed for roughly 300,000 years—consider our ancestor and contemporary Homo erectus.The first human species to walk with modern upright proportions, tame fire, and journey out of Africa all the way to India, China, and Java, *Homo erectus* survived for nearly 1.8 million years—six times longer than our species has existed so far—with its last populations on the island of Java overlapping chronologically with early *Homo sapiens*.

“They did not completely vanish; a piece of them lives on inside the cells of billions of people walking the streets today.” — Svante Pääbo, Nobel Laureate in Physiology or Medicine

The Ancient DNA Revolution: We Met and Interbred

For decades, anthropologists argued over what happened when anatomically modern *Homo sapiens* expanded out of Africa around 60,000 to 70,000 years ago and encountered Neanderthals in the Middle East and Denisovans across Asia. Did we replace them without mixing, or did our families merge?

Svante Pääbo’s Nobel Prize-winning work sequencing ancient genomes settled the question beyond doubt: whenever different human groups met, they interbred.

  • Neanderthal DNA in Modern Humans: Today, almost every person of non-Sub-Saharan African ancestry (across Europe, India, East Asia, and the Americas) carries between 1% and 2.5% Neanderthal DNA in their genome—affecting keratin skin proteins, immune responses to viruses, and even circadian sleep preferences.
  • Denisovan DNA Gifts: Populations across South Asia, East Asia, and Oceania carry Denisovan DNA (reaching up to 4% to 6% in Melanesians and Indigenous Australians). Most remarkably, the genetic mutation (*EPAS1*) that allows modern Tibetans and Sherpas to thrive in thin, low-oxygen air above 4,000 meters in the Himalayas was not evolved from scratch by *Homo sapiens*—it was inherited directly from high-altitude Denisovans who interbred with our ancestors tens of thousands of years ago!

Why Did Only Homo Sapiens Survive?

Why, between 40,000 and 30,000 years ago, did every other human species fade into extinction while *Homo sapiens* went on to build cities, decode DNA, and launch space telescopes? It was not because individual *Sapiens* were stronger or had larger brains than individual Neanderthals.

Anthropologists point to our unique superpower: flexible, large-scale cooperation powered by symbolic language and shared storytelling. Neanderthals lived in tight, self-reliant family bands of 15 to 30 individuals and rarely traded materials beyond a few dozen kilometers. *Homo sapiens*, by contrast, used complex language, art, shells, and shared cultural myths to connect bands of hundreds of strangers across hundreds of kilometers into trade and mutual-aid networks.

When volcanic winters, ice-age climate swings, or local food shortages struck, an isolated clan of twenty Neanderthals could be wiped out; a connected network of five hundred *Homo sapiens* could share tools, swap hunting intelligence, and take shelter with distant allies. We survived—and inherited the Earth—not because we were the fiercest lone predators, but because we were the best collaborators.
