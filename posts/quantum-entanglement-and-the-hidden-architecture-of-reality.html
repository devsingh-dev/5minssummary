Quantum entanglement: Why the universe is not locally real

Albert Einstein called it spooky action at a distance and refused to believe it. Half a century of experiments proved that separated particles really do share an instantaneous cosmic link.

E

Editor

5minSummary Editorial

In our everyday macroscopic world, objects have definite properties whether anyone is looking at them or not. A cricket ball sitting inside a closed wooden cupboard is still spherical, still weighs 160 grams, and still has a red leather seam even in pitch darkness. Furthermore, nothing you do to a ball in Raipur can instantaneously alter the physical state of another ball sitting in London without some physical signal traveling between them at or below the speed of light. Physicists call these two common-sense assumptions realism (objects have pre-existing properties) and locality (influences cannot travel faster than light). Together, they form local realism—the bedrock of classical physics from Isaac Newton to Albert Einstein.

Yet when physicists in the early twentieth century zoomed down to the scale of individual electrons, photons, and atoms, local realism shattered completely. According to the mathematical rules of quantum mechanics, two subatomic particles can become entangled—bound together in a single shared mathematical wave function—such that measuring one particle instantaneously determines the state of its partner, even if the two particles are on opposite sides of the galaxy.

This idea disturbed Albert Einstein so deeply that he spent the final decades of his life arguing that quantum mechanics must be incomplete. The resulting eighty-year debate between Einstein and Niels Bohr—and the ingenious experiments that finally settled it—forced humanity to accept that the universe at its deepest level is far stranger than common sense allows.

What Does It Actually Mean for Two Particles to Be Entangled?

To picture quantum entanglement without getting lost in abstract equations, imagine a subatomic property called spin (or the polarization of a photon of light). In the classical world, if you take a pair of shoes, place the left shoe in one box and the right shoe in another box, and mail one box to Mumbai and the other to Tokyo, there is no mystery. The moment the person in Mumbai opens their box and sees a left shoe, they instantly know the box in Tokyo contains the right shoe. The shoes had their identities all along; opening the box merely revealed pre-existing information.

Quantum particles do not behave like shoes in boxes. Before a measurement is made, an entangled electron does not have a secret, pre-assigned spin pointing “up” or “down.” Instead, it exists in a quantum superposition—a blurry probability cloud of both up and down simultaneously, like a coin spinning in mid-air before it lands on the table.

When two electrons become entangled with a total spin of zero, neither particle has a definite direction on its own. Both coins are spinning in mid-air as they fly miles apart. Yet the exact microsecond an experimenter slaps a detector down on Particle A and forces it to land on “Heads (Spin Up),” Particle B—even if it is light-years away—instantaneously collapses into “Tails (Spin Down)” without any time delay.

“I cannot seriously believe in quantum theory because it cannot be reconciled with the idea that physics should represent a reality in time and space, free from spooky actions at a distance.” — Albert Einstein, 1947

The EPR Paradox and Hidden Variables

In 1935, Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen published a famous paper known as the EPR Paradox. They argued that quantum superposition had to be an illusion. Surely, Einstein reasoned, entangled particles are just like the left and right shoes: at the moment they are created, they must be stamped with secret internal instructions—what physicists call local hidden variables—that dictate in advance how they will behave when measured later. If we simply haven’t discovered those hidden variables yet, that is a limitation of our instruments, not proof of telepathic particles.

For nearly thirty years, the argument between Einstein (hidden variables) and Niels Bohr (true quantum indeterminacy) remained purely philosophical. Because both explanations predicted the exact same 50/50 outcome when detectors were pointed in the same direction, there seemed to be no way to test in a laboratory whether the particle’s spin was decided at birth or at the moment of measurement.

John Stewart Bell’s Mathematical Masterstroke

In 1964, a brilliant Northern Irish physicist named John Stewart Bell, working while on leave from CERN, discovered a way to bring the debate out of philosophy and into the laboratory. Bell realized that while hidden variables and quantum mechanics make identical predictions when two detectors are aligned at 0 degrees or 90 degrees, they make mathematically different statistical predictions when the detectors are tilted at intermediate angles—such as 22.5 degrees or 45 degrees.

Bell derived a simple mathematical formula now known as Bell’s Inequality. If particles carry pre-existing hidden instructions (like Einstein believed), the correlation between millions of paired measurements at tilted angles can never exceed a strict numerical ceiling. If, however, particles exist in genuine quantum superposition until the instant of measurement (as Bohr argued), the correlations will break right through Bell’s ceiling.

Closing Every Loophole: Aspect, Clauser, and Zeilinger

Starting in the 1970s and 1980s, experimental physicists John Clauser in the United States and Alain Aspect in France built delicate optical laboratories that fired entangled pairs of photons through polarization filters switched at millones of times per second while the photons were already in flight. Later, Anton Zeilinger in Austria extended these tests across dozens of kilometers and even used ancient starlight from distant quasars billions of light-years away to set the detector angles randomly.

Every single experiment produced the exact same verdict: Bell’s Inequality was violated. Einstein, for once in his extraordinary career, was wrong. Particles do not carry secret hidden instructions. The universe is not locally real—meaning that physical properties do not exist in a definite state until they interact with the rest of the world, and entangled systems behave as a single indivisible whole regardless of the space separating them.

Why Doesn’t Entanglement Violate the Speed of Light?

A natural question immediately arises: if measuring Particle A instantaneously affects Particle B across the galaxy, why can’t we use quantum entanglement to build a faster-than-light Morse code transmitter? The answer lies in the No-Communication Theorem.

  • Random Individual Outcomes: When Alice measures her entangled particle on Earth, she cannot force it to land on “Spin Up” or “Spin Down.” She gets a completely random 50/50 sequence of 1s and 0s.
  • Correlation Requires Classical Comparison: Bob, sitting on Mars, also sees what looks like a completely random 50/50 sequence of 1s and 0s on his detector. It is only *later*, when Alice sends a regular radio signal traveling at the speed of light to compare her notebook with Bob’s notebook, that they discover their two random lists are exact mirror images of each other. No usable message can travel faster than light.

How Entanglement Powers the Technology of 2026

Far from being an esoteric curiosity, quantum entanglement is now the core resource driving a technological revolution:

  • Unhackable Quantum Cryptography (QKD): Satellites and fiber-optic networks now use entangled photons to distribute encryption keys. Because any attempt by a hacker to intercept and measure an entangled photon immediately collapses its quantum state and alerts both sender and receiver, quantum key distribution is protected not by mathematical complexity, but by the fundamental laws of physics.
  • Quantum Computing and Qubits: Classical computers process information in binary bits that must be either 0 or 1. Quantum computers link dozens or hundreds of entangled qubits together. While 300 classical bits can hold only one 300-digit binary number at a time, 300 fully entangled qubits can represent $2^{300}$ states simultaneously—more states than there are atoms in the observable universe—allowing scientists to simulate complex molecules, catalysts, and materials from first principles.
