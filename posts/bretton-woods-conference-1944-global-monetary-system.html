The 1944 Bretton Woods Conference: How 44 Nations Designed the Modern Global Economy

While World War II still raged in July 1944, 730 delegates met at a remote New Hampshire hotel to build the IMF, the World Bank, and the U.S. Dollar’s reign as global reserve currency.

5minSummary Editorial

On July 1, 1944—less than four weeks after Allied troops stormed the beaches of Normandy on D-Day and while World War II still raged across Europe and the Pacific—730 economists, finance ministers, and central bankers from 44 Allied nations (including a five-member Indian delegation led by Sir Jeremy Raisman, Sir Chintaman Deshmukh—the first Indian Governor of the Reserve Bank of India—Sir R. K. Shanmukham Chetty, and A. D. Shroff) boarded special trains to the remote Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire.

Why hold a three-week monetary summit in the middle of a world war? Because Allied leaders were haunted by the economic disaster of the 1930s Great Depression. After the 1929 crash, countries had waged destructive “Beggar-Thy-Neighbor” trade wars—slashing the value of their currencies to undercut rivals and erecting punitive tariff walls (like the 1930 U.S. Smoot-Hawley Tariff). World trade collapsed by 66% between 1929 and 1934, breeding mass unemployment, fascism, and ultimately World War II. The delegates at Bretton Woods were determined to build a rule-based global financial architecture that would make another 1930s-style collapse impossible.

The Intellectual Duel: John Maynard Keynes vs. Harry Dexter White

The conference quickly became a historic clash of economic visions between the two dominant Western powers—a financially exhausted British Empire and a surging United States that now held nearly two-thirds of the entire world’s official gold reserves:

  • 1. The British Vision (John Maynard Keynes & the “Bancor”): Britain’s delegation was led by Lord John Maynard Keynes, the world’s most famous economist. Keynes knew Britain was emerging from the war deeply indebted. He proposed creating an international clearing union with a neutral, supranational world reserve currency called the “Bancor”—a system that would penalize countries running huge trade surpluses (like the US) just as much as countries running trade deficits, preventing any single nation’s domestic currency from dominating the globe.
  • 2. The American Vision (Harry Dexter White & the Gold-Backed U.S. Dollar): The U.S. Treasury delegation was led by chief international economist Harry Dexter White. Because the United States possessed both the factories and the gold that the post-war world desperately needed, White insisted that the new global monetary system be anchored not on an artificial “Bancor,” but directly on the United States Dollar, backed by physical gold inside Fort Knox.

With American economic leverage overwhelming, White’s blueprint carried the day—establishing the Bretton Woods System signed on July 22, 1944.

The Three Pillars of the Bretton Woods Architecture

The agreement erected three foundational pillars that governed post-war global capitalism:

Pillar 1: The Adjustable Peg ($35 per Ounce of Gold)

Instead of wild, daily currency devaluations, every member country agreed to peg its national currency (Pound, Franc, Mark, Yen, Rupee) at a fixed exchange rate to the U.S. Dollar (allowed to fluctuate within a narrow ±1% band). In turn, the United States Government pledged to convert U.S. Dollars held by foreign central banks into physical gold at a rock-solid fixed price of $35.00 per troy ounce of gold. Overnight, the U.S. Dollar became the world’s undisputed Global Reserve Currency—considered *“as good as gold.”*

Pillar 2: The International Monetary Fund (IMF)

To prevent countries from devaluing their currencies whenever they hit a temporary trade deficit, Bretton Woods created the International Monetary Fund (IMF)—an emergency pool of gold and national currencies that acted as a global financial firefighter, lending short-term bridge funds to stabilize exchange rates.

Pillar 3: The World Bank (IBRD) & GATT

To finance the physical reconstruction of war-torn Europe and Asia and fund dams, railways, and power grids in developing nations, the conference established the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD)—known today as the World Bank (soon complemented in 1947 by the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, GATT, the precursor to the World Trade Organization, WTO).

The Triffin Dilemma and the “Nixon Shock” of August 15, 1941

For twenty-five years, the Bretton Woods system powered the Post-War Economic Golden Age (1945–1970), as global trade, manufacturing in Western Europe and Japan, and living standards soared. Yet in 1960, Belgian-American economist Robert Triffin testified before the U.S. Congress warning of a fatal mathematical flaw built into the system—now famous as the Triffin Dilemma:

  • To supply fast-growing global trade with enough U.S. Dollars, the United States had to print more dollars and send them abroad via trade deficits and overseas military spending.
  • However, the more dollars the U.S. printed, the more total overseas dollars exceeded the actual physical gold sitting in U.S. vaults at $35/oz, eventually destroying foreign confidence that the U.S. could honor its gold pledge!

By 1965–1971, as U.S. spending on the Vietnam War and Great Society programs swelled, French President Charles de Gaulle and European central banks began sending warships and cables to exchange their paper U.S. Dollars for physical American gold. With U.S. gold reserves plunging toward the danger line, President Richard Nixon went on live television on the evening of August 15, 1971 (the “Nixon Shock”) and unilaterally closed the gold window, ending the convertibility of the U.S. Dollar into gold.

Although the fixed $35 gold peg ended between 1971 and 1973—ushering in today’s era of floating fiat currencies—the institutional framework born at Bretton Woods (the IMF, the World Bank, the WTO, and the U.S. Dollar’s deep network role in global commerce) remains the operating system of international finance to this day.
