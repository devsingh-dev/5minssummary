What Is a Credit Score and How Does It Work? The Complete Global Guide

A three-digit number quietly decides whether you pay $180,000 or $260,000 for the exact same home. Inside the mathematical formula banks use to judge your financial trust.

5minSummary Editorial

Imagine two coworkers, Maya and Liam, walking into the exact same bank on the same Tuesday morning to apply for a $350,000 thirty-year home mortgage. Both earn $85,000 a year, both work at the same technology company, and both have saved a 20% down payment. Yet the bank offers Maya an interest rate of 6.1%, while offering Liam a rate of 7.6%. Over the life of the loan, Liam will pay $124,900 more in pure interest for the exact same house—simply because Maya’s three-digit credit score is 780, while Liam’s is 635.

Before the late 1950s, getting a loan depended on personal connections—and the subjective prejudices—of a small-town bank manager. In 1956, engineer Bill Fair and mathematician Earl Isaac founded the Fair Isaac Corporation (FICO) with a revolutionary idea: replace human bias with an objective, statistical algorithm that predicts the probability that a borrower will repay a debt on time over the next 24 months. Today, whether it is called a FICO Score / VantageScore in the United States, a CIBIL Score in India, or an Experian / Equifax / TransUnion score in the UK, Canada, and Australia, this three-digit number is your adult financial report card.

Credit Report vs. Credit Score: Understanding the Difference

Many consumers confuse a *credit report* with a *credit score*, but they are two distinct things:

  • Your Credit Report (The Raw History): Maintained by licensed credit bureaus—primarily Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion globally (plus TransUnion CIBIL and CRIF High Mark in India)—your credit report is a detailed multi-page log of every credit card, auto loan, student loan, and mortgage you have held over the past 7 to 10 years, including every monthly payment status and credit limit.
  • Your Credit Score (The Mathematical Grade): A credit score is a three-digit number (typically ranging from 300 to 850 in the FICO/VantageScore system, or 300 to 900 in India’s CIBIL system) generated when a scoring algorithm reads the raw data inside your credit report at a specific moment in time.

Standard FICO / Global Credit Score Tiers (300–850 Scale)

  • 800 – 850 (Exceptional / Super-Prime): Unlocks the lowest advertised interest rates, instant credit card approvals, and premium rewards.
  • 740 – 799 (Very Good): Qualifies for near-top-tier mortgage and auto loan rates with minimal friction.
  • 670 – 739 (Good / Prime): Considered an acceptable, mainstream borrower by the vast majority of banks.
  • 580 – 669 (Fair / Near-Prime): Approved by lenders, but charged significantly higher interest rates and stricter terms.
  • 300 – 579 (Poor / Subprime): High probability of loan rejection or requirement of a cash-backed secured deposit.

The Exact 5-Factor Mathematical Formula Behind Your Score

According to official disclosures from myFICO (Fair Isaac Corporation)—whose scores are used in over 90% of U.S. lending decisions—your credit score is calculated from five weighted categories:

1. Payment History — 35% of Your Score

The single biggest question a bank asks is: *Have you paid your past bills on time?* Even a single payment that is more than 30 days late reported to the bureaus can drop a high 780 credit score by 60 to 100 points overnight. Payments that go 60 days, 90 days, or into collections, charge-offs, or bankruptcy cause severe damage that stays on your report for seven years (ten years for Chapter 7 bankruptcy in the US).

2. Credit Utilization Ratio (Amounts Owed) — 30% of Your Score

This is the second largest factor—and the easiest one to manipulate quickly. Credit utilization is the percentage of your total available revolving credit card limit that you are currently using:

Credit Utilization (%) = (Total Credit Card Statement Balances ÷ Total Credit Card Limits) × 100

For example, if you have a credit card with a $10,000 (or ₹1,00,000) limit and your monthly statement closes with a $5,000 (₹50,000) balance, your utilization is 50%—even if you pay the bill in full on the due date! While banking wisdom says to stay under 30% utilization, FICO data shows that borrowers with 800+ scores keep their reported utilization between 1% and 9%.

“Paying your credit card in full on the due date saves you from paying interest, but paying your balance down two days *before* the monthly statement closing date is what skyrockets your credit score.” — Consumer Credit Scoring Mechanics

3. Length of Credit History — 15% of Your Score

Algorithms reward long-term track records. This factor looks at the age of your oldest open account, the age of your newest account, and the average age of all your accounts. That is why closing your oldest no-annual-fee credit card is a classic mistake: it shrinks your total available credit limit and eventually lowers your average account age.

4. New Credit & Hard Inquiries — 10% of Your Score

Whenever you apply for a new credit card or loan, the lender performs a Hard Inquiry (Hard Pull) on your credit report, which typically trims 3 to 5 points off your score for a few months. Applying for six credit cards in two months signals financial distress to the algorithm. *(Note: Checking your own credit score on banking apps is a Soft Inquiry and has zero impact on your score!)*

5. Credit Mix — 10% of Your Score

Lenders like to see that you can responsibly handle both revolving credit (credit cards where the balance fluctuates monthly) and installment loans (fixed monthly payments like an auto loan, student loan, or mortgage).

4 Common Credit Score Myths Debunked by Data

  • Myth 1: “You need to carry a monthly balance and pay interest to build a credit score.” *False.* Carrying a balance costs you 20% to 42% APR and hurts your utilization ratio. Paying your statement in full every month builds a top-tier score for $0 in interest.
  • Myth 2: “A higher salary automatically gives you a higher credit score.” *False.* Your income, bank savings balance, and net worth are not even listed on your credit report! A millionaire who forgets to pay a $40 credit card bill for 60 days will have a lower score than a college student who pays a $200 card on time every month.
  • Myth 3: “Using a Debit Card or UPI builds your credit score.” *False.* Debit cards and instant bank transfers spend your own cash; because no credit is extended, they are never reported to credit bureaus.
  • Myth 4: “Shopping around for the best mortgage or auto loan rate will ruin your score with multiple hard inquiries.” *False.* Modern FICO and VantageScore models include a Rate-Shopping Window (14 to 45 days): all mortgage or auto loan inquiries made within a 30-day window are grouped together and counted as just one single inquiry.
