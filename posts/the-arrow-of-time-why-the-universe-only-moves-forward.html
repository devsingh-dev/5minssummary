The arrow of time: Why we remember the past, never the future, and why time flows differently across space

Almost every fundamental equation in physics works equally well backward and forward. Only one law of nature explains why coffee cools, glass shatters, and yesterday never returns.

E

Editor

5minSummary Editorial

Nothing feels more obvious to human experience than the flow of time. We watch an ice cube melt in a glass of water, never a puddle of lukewarm water spontaneously assembling itself into a sharp cube of ice. We remember what we ate for breakfast yesterday, yet we have no memory of what we will eat tomorrow. We grow older year by year, never younger. In the words of British astrophysicist Arthur Eddington, our lives are governed by an unmistakable, one-way Arrow of Time.

Yet here is one of the deepest mysteries in all of science: if you open a textbook of fundamental physics and examine the equations that govern the microscopic building blocks of the universe—Newton’s laws of motion, Maxwell’s equations of electromagnetism, Einstein’s general relativity, or Erwin Schrödinger’s wave equation of quantum mechanics—almost none of them distinguish between the past and the future.

If you filmed two billiard balls (or two individual atoms) colliding and bouncing off each other, and then played that video in reverse for a physicist, the reversed video would obey every single law of microscopic physics just as perfectly as the forward video. At the level of individual atoms, time is symmetric. So where does the relentless forward flow of time in our macroscopic world come from?

Ludwig Boltzmann and the Law of Entropy

Across the entire edifice of physics, there is only one fundamental law that points in a single direction from past to future: the Second Law of Thermodynamics, which states that the total entropy of an isolated system always increases (or remains constant) over time.

In everyday conversation, people often define entropy loosely as “disorder” or “chaos.” In the 1870s, however, the brilliant Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann discovered the true mathematical meaning of entropy—an insight so profound that his formula, S = k log W, is carved onto his tombstone in Vienna.

Boltzmann realized that entropy is simply a game of probability and counting microstates:

  • Imagine a brand-new deck of 52 playing cards arranged in perfect numerical and suit order from Ace of Spades to King of Diamonds. There is only 1 specific arrangement that counts as “perfectly ordered.”
  • Now throw that deck of cards into the air and gather them up. How many possible disordered arrangements exist? Roughly 8 × 10⁶⁷ (an 8 followed by 67 zeros)! If you shuffle an ordered deck, it becomes disordered not because a mysterious cosmic force loves chaos, but simply because there are quadrillions of times more ways for the cards to be scrambled than ordered.
“An egg shatters on the kitchen floor and never jumps back together onto the table for a simple statistical reason: there are vastly more ways for atoms to be a broken egg than an intact one.” — Thermodynamics Primer

Think about a drop of blue ink falling into a glass of clear water, or hot tea cooling down in a room. When all the fast-moving heat molecules are concentrated inside the teacup, that is a rare, low-entropy state (like the unshuffled deck of cards). As those molecules bounce randomly against the cooler air molecules in the room, the heat spreads out evenly until the tea and the room reach the same temperature. Nothing in Newton’s laws forbids all the random air molecules in the room from accidentally colliding with the teacup at the exact same instant to boil the tea again—it is just so astronomically improbable that you would have to wait longer than the age of the universe squared to see it happen once.

The Past Hypothesis: Why Did the Universe Start So Neat?

Boltzmann’s statistical explanation solved half the puzzle, but it immediately raised an even deeper cosmological question: if entropy is always increasing today because the universe is moving from a low-entropy state toward a high-entropy state, why was the universe in such an unimaginably neat, low-entropy state at the Big Bang 13.8 billion years ago?

Cosmologists call this the Past Hypothesis. Shortly after the Big Bang, matter was distributed in a nearly uniform, glowing soup of hot gas. In a system without gravity, a uniform gas has high entropy. But when gravity dominates on a cosmic scale, the rules flip: a smooth, evenly spread cloud of gas actually has *very low gravitational entropy*, because gravity wants to pull matter together into clumps!

As gravity pulled that smooth primordial gas into dense stars, galaxies, and ultimately black holes, it unlocked a colossal downhill waterfall of entropy. Every process that sustains life on Earth—from the sunlight powering our crops to the neurons firing in your brain as you read this sentence—rides like a waterwheel on that grand cosmic cascade from the low-entropy Big Bang toward the high-entropy future.

Einstein’s Twist: Time Does Not Tick at the Same Rate Everywhere

As if thermodynamic time were not fascinating enough, Albert Einstein revolutionized our understanding of time a second time in 1905 (Special Relativity) and 1915 (General Relativity). Before Einstein, Isaac Newton assumed that a single universal clock ticked at the exact same rate everywhere in the cosmos. Einstein proved that time is elastic, personal, and woven together with space into a four-dimensional fabric called spacetime.

How fast your personal clock ticks depends on two physical factors:

1. Velocity Time Dilation (Moving Clocks Tick Slower)

The faster you move through space relative to a stationary observer, the slower you move through time. While imperceptible at highway speeds, this effect has been verified to fifteen decimal places using atomic clocks placed on commercial airplanes and particle accelerators.

2. Gravitational Time Dilation (Gravity Slows Time)

According to General Relativity, massive objects like stars and planets warp the fabric of spacetime around them, and time runs slower the closer you are to a source of gravity. This is not science fiction; it happens right across your own body! An optical strontium atomic clock placed at your feet ticks measurably slower than an identical atomic clock held at the level of your head, because your feet are slightly closer to Earth’s gravitational center.

In fact, every time you use Google Maps or GPS navigation on your smartphone, your phone has to apply Einstein’s relativity equations. The GPS satellites orbiting 20,200 kilometers above Earth experience weaker gravity than we do on the ground, causing their onboard atomic clocks to tick 38 microseconds faster every single day than clocks on Earth. If engineers did not program Einstein’s time-dilation correction into the satellite software, GPS locations on your phone would drift off course by 10 kilometers every single day!

Living Inside the Present Moment

In Einstein’s four-dimensional “Block Universe,” past, present, and future all have physical validity as coordinates in spacetime, yet as biological creatures powered by thermodynamics, we experience reality as a moving window of consciousness converting future possibilities into past memories. Time is neither an illusion nor a rigid cage—it is the dynamic canvas that allows change, growth, and life to exist.
